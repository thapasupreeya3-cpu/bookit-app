Disability and Mental Health Care Pty Ltd | Feedback, Complaints and Incident Considerations

These guidelines detail procedural fairness considerations for handling complaints and feedback involving allegations of inappropriate conduct by a worker.

Assess information confidentiality

The very first step to manage a complaint or feedback involves identifying any confidential information associated with the issue. It is crucial to uphold and maintain confidentiality throughout the process to ensure that sensitive details are not revealed without necessity. This also helps maintain a level of trust and integrity in the procedure. If any complexities related to the disclosure of such information arise, it is recommended to seek advice from the person who provided the information or from a legal expert.

Examine the workers involved

If the allegations involve more than one worker, it is essential to handle each case separately with equal respect and procedural fairness. The seriousness of the allegations, the individual circumstances, and the impact on the work environment should be considered when deciding the process for each case.

Choose the complaint manager

The selection of a neutral party within the organisation to handle the complaint can greatly influence the fairness of the process. The person or persons chosen should have no personal or professional bias toward any party involved in the allegations. They should be sufficiently trained to handle such sensitive matters, have excellent interpersonal and conflict resolution skills, and ensure that no conflicts of interest exist in their role as complaint managers.

Review workplace systems

As part of the investigation, it is advisable to review any relevant workplace training, policies, procedures, and work systems that could be related to the allegations. This step can help identify any systemic issues that could be contributing to the problem, as well as provide context for the investigation.

Plan the investigation process

The complaint management process should be tailored to the specific situation, considering factors such as the need for confidentiality, the individuals involved, and the choice of complaint manager. A well-planned and clearly defined process can contribute to procedural fairness and ensure that all parties understand the steps involved.

The following six-step strategy is versatile for investigating complaints by a complaint manager, incident manager or external investigator.

- Assessment: understand the complaint, considering all perspectives involved. If clarity is needed, contact the complainant directly. Act promptly to resolve or escalate the complaint, keeping all involved parties informed. Involve Workers, track time, and consider privacy and communication needs.
- Investigation: familiarise yourself with relevant policies and processes. Share complaint details confidentially, collect documents, allocate time for thorough investigation, and outline the problem's chronology. Identify disputes and areas for further investigation and obtain comments from involved parties. Keep records, conduct interviews tactfully, promise realistically, and keep complainants informed about delays.
- Weighing the evidence: evaluate all evidence. Look for incorrect information, policy gaps, or procedural failures. Consider legislative guidelines, internal policies, service charters, precedents, and service agreements. If evidence is inconclusive, further interviews or advice may be required. Keep complainants informed about delays and maintain transparency.
- Correcting mistakes: identify and rectify mistakes, take steps to avoid recurrence, and communicate findings to the team. Mistakes can range from poor service to failure in complying with regulations.
- Putting things right: take actions to improve services. Remedial actions may include apologies, explanations, reviews, staff training, performance improvement, or financial compensation. Assess financial redress considering the complainant's actions, incurred costs, lost benefits, inconvenience, professional advice costs, and time spent on the complaint.
- Respond: reassure complainants with a well-crafted response showing understanding and commitment. If writing, provide a clear definition of the complaint, explain findings and decisions, and offer good news first. Close with a goodwill statement and provide information for complaint escalation.

While not all complaints necessitate full investigation, complex or serious ones, especially those involving serious misconduct allegations against a specific worker, do.

Inform the worker

The worker or workers involved should be informed of the allegations against them and the process that will be followed to investigate these. This communication should be clear, comprehensive, and in writing, particularly in serious cases. This step helps ensure that the worker is fully aware of the situation and their rights during the investigation.

Discuss potential sanctions

The worker should also be informed about possible outcomes or sanctions if the allegations are substantiated. These could range from formal actions such as dismissal or a formal investigation, to informal actions such as changes to work duties or a negative report in their record. By discussing these possibilities, the worker is given a chance to understand the seriousness of the allegations and the potential implications.

Allow the worker's response

The worker should be given a fair chance to respond to allegations and proposed sanctions, which can be through a meeting, a written statement, or via a representative such as a lawyer or union official. There may be a need for multiple statements or submissions to address all issues. In severe cases, separate procedures for assessing allegations and determining sanctions could be beneficial. Special accommodations like time extensions should be considered when necessary to ensure fairness.

Record the decision

Once the investigation is completed, the decision should be communicated to the worker in writing. This not only provides a formal record of the process and the findings but also helps to ensure transparency and fairness. If a sanction is imposed, clearly state its nature and explain the procedure if the worker wishes to contest it.

Ensure a fair investigation

The investigation should be carried out impartially and fairly, with no preconceived notions held by the investigator. All evidence must be meticulously reviewed, and every party involved treated with respect and fairness. Consider having someone other than the investigator make decisions about sanctions, if any, to avoid potential bias or prejudgment at this stage. This separation between the investigation and sanctioning roles can further ensure unbiased treatment and fairness in the process.

Maintain an overall fair process

Procedural fairness governs the whole complaint resolution process instead of just individual stages. It is essential that individuals are provided with a suitable opportunity to address any negative findings or sanctions, even if this comes at a later phase of the process. Early mistakes in the process can be amended subsequently, assuming an open-minded and good faith approach is adopted.

Even when some decisions seem to disadvantage a party, if the overall process is executed in an impartial, transparent, and fair manner, the principles of procedural fairness are upheld. Recognising the dual role of procedural fairness is vital: it is a legal obligation for decisions affecting individuals under statute, and it simultaneously works to enhance the fairness and integrity of administrative procedures, regardless of the potential for any legal repercussions due to non-compliance.

Handle confidential information

Ensure utmost respect for the confidentiality of the information involved in the complaint. Maintaining the confidentiality of such information protects the rights of all involved parties, supports trust in the investigation process, and preserves the integrity of the workplace environment. However, procedural fairness might supersede confidentiality under certain conditions or legal requirements. The disclosure of such sensitive information should only occur if it is legally mandated or contextually appropriate in light of the allegations.

In situations where the complaints revolve around the quality of support, rather than allegations of worker misconduct, confidentiality tends to be more easily maintained. It is often possible to share allegation specifics without disclosing the sources, though in some scenarios fairness may necessitate revealing a source. The decision-maker should strive for a balance between fairness and confidentiality, and may need to conduct preliminary discussions with all parties involved to navigate this balance.

Eligibility to manage a complaint or incident

The following are the criteria for a complaint or incident manager to be eligible to handle feedback, complaints or an incident. A complaint manager or incident manager, especially in an NDIS provider like Disability and Mental Health Care Pty Ltd, must approach their duties with neutrality, free from actual or perceived bias. This requirement ensures the integrity and legal validity of their decision-making process and outcomes. A fair-minded observer should not reasonably suspect any partiality from them.

Identifying bias

Potential bias could be inferred from the individual's conduct, remarks, relationships, or any other pertinent circumstances. Some examples of situations that may suggest bias include:

- having a personal interest or conflict of interest in the matter to be resolved, or a relationship with one of the parties that could compromise the appearance of fairness;
- showing favouritism or hostility towards any of the parties involved;
- making comments indicating a prejudgment of a disputed issue, or a lack of willingness to examine the evidence impartially; and
- involvement in an earlier stage of the process, such as making the allegation to be investigated or providing a statement supporting one of the parties.

Delegating responsibility

In cases of actual or perceived bias, it is the responsibility of the complaint manager or incident manager to step aside from the process and delegate decision-making responsibility to another person. If this is not feasible, for instance in smaller organisations, the inquiry or assessment role could be outsourced to an external professional who would then prepare a report for the complaint manager or incident manager.

Addressing issues of bias or conflict of interest

Potential issues of bias and conflict of interest should be addressed upfront, before the process begins. The parties involved can then, upon being informed of a potential issue, choose to waive any objection and allow the complaint manager or incident manager to proceed. However, an objection by a party should not automatically disqualify a complaint manager or incident manager; the reasonable perception of a fair-minded observer should be the guiding principle. It is common for decision-makers to know or work with one or more of the parties, have familiarity with the issues to be decided, or express preliminary views on those issues.

Handover of an investigation

If a bias issue emerges during the inquiry, after evidence and submissions have been collected, these could generally be handed over to the new decision-maker, ensuring fairness in the process. As a good practice, the parties should be consulted about this option beforehand.
